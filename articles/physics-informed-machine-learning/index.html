Physics-informed machine learning: from physical laws to neural solvers

How physical knowledge shapes learning, how PINNs solve differential equations, and how to check whether a learned solution is reliable.

Learning from measurements and physical laws

A temperature sensor tells us what happened at one place and time. A heat equation tells us how temperature can change throughout a domain. Physics-informed machine learning brings these two sources of knowledge into the same model: observations constrain it where measurements exist, while physical structure restricts what it can predict elsewhere.

The physical knowledge might be a differential equation, a conservation law, a symmetry, a known boundary value, or a restriction such as positivity. It can shape the training data, the network architecture, or the training objective. This is a broad family of methods rather than one design: a network that respects rotational symmetry and a network trained on a differential-equation residual both use physics, in different ways.

Why is this useful? Measurements can be sparse or expensive, and an unconstrained model can fit them while still predicting physically inconsistent behaviour. A valid physical model narrows the range of plausible solutions. It cannot repair an incorrect governing equation, remove measurement bias, or guarantee reliable predictions outside the validated conditions.

From a neural network to a differential-equation solver

A physics-informed neural network, or PINN, represents an unknown physical field with a trainable function. For a time-dependent problem, the inputs can be position and time; the output can be temperature, velocity, pressure, or a quantum-state component.

uθ(x,t)≈u(x,t)

Here, theta denotes the trainable weights and biases. A conventional supervised model learns only from pairs of coordinates and known field values. A PINN can also check how well its predictions satisfy a governing equation at coordinates where the solution is unknown. These locations are called collocation points; they carry no solution labels.

Automatic differentiation applies the chain rule through the network to obtain derivatives with respect to its inputs, which enter the equation residual. Training then differentiates the loss with respect to the network parameters. These are two distinct uses of differentiation, and neither makes the approximate solution exact.

The early PINN formulation by Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis describes this route to differentiable solutions. In a standard forward problem, the coefficients and the initial and boundary conditions are given, and the network is optimized for that one problem; a new parameter setting can require another training run. Learning a map from whole families of inputs to solutions is operator learning, a different setup.

A concrete example: diffusion along a rod

Consider a one-dimensional rod whose ends are held at zero temperature relative to a reference. Use dimensionless position and time, a positive diffusion coefficient, and a sine-shaped initial temperature:

∂u∂t=α∂2u∂x2, α > 0
u(x,0)=sin(πx),u(0,t)=u(1,t)=0

Position and time both run from zero to one, and the figures use a diffusion coefficient of 0.1. Diffusion smooths the profile and transfers heat to the fixed-temperature ends. The exact solution is:

u(x,t)=e−απ2t⁢sin(πx)

This analytic solution lets us explain and test the method without confusing a reference curve with a trained prediction. The plot below contains no PINN training result.

A PINN replaces the unknown temperature with its network output. Substituting that output into the heat equation gives the residual:

rθ(x,t)=∂uθ∂t−α∂2uθ∂x2

The residual vanishes for an exact solution. A nonzero value measures a local violation of the equation; its magnitude is not, by itself, the error in temperature.

Building the loss function

Four kinds of information can contribute to training: the equation in the interior, the initial state, the boundary values, and any observations. A common soft-constraint formulation uses a weighted sum:

ℒ=λrℒr+λiℒi+λbℒb+λdℒd

For this example, the individual terms are:

ℒr=1Nr∑j=1Nr|rθ(xj,tj)|2ℒi=1Ni∑j=1Ni|uθ(xj,0)−sin(πxj)|2ℒb=1Nb∑j=1Nb|uθ(xj,tj)|2ℒd=1Nd∑j=1Nd|uθ(xj,tj)−yj|2

Each sum averages over its own point set. Residual points sample the interior in space and time, initial points lie at time zero, and boundary points lie at either end of the rod, where the target temperature is zero. Observation points carry measured temperatures, denoted by y.

The weights control the relative contribution of each term. They need care, because different quantities and derivatives can have very different scales. Nondimensionalization and normalization make optimization easier to interpret, and equal numerical weights do not imply equal influence on the parameter gradients.

Observations are optional for a fully specified forward problem; without them, simply omit that term. In an inverse problem, observations can also help identify unknown equation coefficients. Recovering a coefficient requires informative measurements and an identifiable model; a small fitting error does not establish a unique answer.

Soft penalties and exact constraints

A soft penalty encourages a condition but generally does not enforce it exactly. For the rod, a different representation imposes both the initial profile and the zero boundary values by construction:

uθ(x,t)=sin(πx)+tx(1−x)Nθ(x,t)

For a finite, smooth network output, the extra term vanishes at time zero and at both endpoints. This construction is specific to these compatible conditions; complex geometries or boundary operators need different ones. A hard constraint simplifies one part of training but can change the conditioning of the rest.

Why the equation alone is insufficient

The identically zero temperature satisfies the heat equation and the zero boundary conditions, but not the sine-shaped initial condition. A network that collapses to zero can therefore reach zero equation residual while solving the wrong problem.

Residual, constraint error, observation error, and solution error measure different things and must be checked together. Even a small combined training loss is a finite-sample observation, not a proof of accuracy throughout the domain.

What happens during training?

  1. Specify the physical problem: field variables, domain, equation, and compatible initial and boundary conditions. Scale the variables and state which coefficients are known.
  2. Choose a representation, and decide whether some constraints will be imposed exactly. Strong-form residuals need enough differentiability; smooth activations are a natural start for a second-order equation.
  3. Sample interior, initial, boundary, and optional observation points, and reserve independent points for validation.
  4. Evaluate the network and its coordinate derivatives, and assemble each residual or mismatch.
  5. Differentiate the weighted loss with respect to the parameters and take an optimizer step. Repeat while monitoring the separate loss terms.
  6. Validate independently: evaluate errors away from the training points, inspect the conditions, and compare with an analytic solution or a trusted numerical solver. Repeat with different samples or initializations when reliability matters.

For the rod, a useful check is the relative discrete L2 error on a separate space-time grid:

Erel=∑(uθ(zj)−u(zj))2∑u(zj)2

This compares values on the chosen evaluation grid; it is not a continuous-domain certificate. Check maximum errors and boundary mismatches too, because an average can hide a localized failure.

Where physics-informed learning helps

PINNs provide a differentiable representation that can be evaluated anywhere in the modelled domain. They can combine sparse observations with equations, couple a forward model to unknown parameters, and place control objectives alongside physical constraints. This makes inverse problems, data assimilation, and constrained design natural settings to investigate.

In fluid dynamics, physical constraints can relate sparse flow measurements. In heat transfer, they connect temperatures to transport parameters. In quantum dynamics, they constrain state evolution while learning controls. In relativistic physics, they can encode coupled field equations or eigenvalue problems. Each application still needs a problem-specific representation, sampling strategy, and independent reference checks.

Conventional finite-difference, finite-volume, finite-element, and spectral solvers remain essential baselines. For many well-posed forward problems, they are faster, more accurate, or easier to certify. A fair comparison includes training time, inference time, hardware, accuracy, and how often the trained model is reused.

Limitations that matter in practice

Optimization can fail even when a network can represent the desired solution. Loss terms can compete, and differential operators can make training poorly conditioned. Krishnapriyan and colleagues demonstrate such failure modes for convection, reaction, and diffusion problems, and investigate curriculum and sequential formulations.

Sharp fronts, shocks, multiple scales, high frequencies, and long time intervals are difficult. A strong-form residual is particularly delicate at a discontinuity, where the classical derivative may not exist; weak formulations, domain decomposition, or conservative methods may be more appropriate.

Automatic differentiation can be costly for higher derivatives or large collocation batches, and sparse sampling can miss narrow regions of error. A mis-specified physical model biases predictions, and a deterministic network does not provide calibrated uncertainty. Physics-informed training offers useful constraints, not a general guarantee of convergence, extrapolation, or computational advantage.

A practical starting point

Begin with a problem whose solution you can check independently. Write down the units, the domain, and every required condition before designing the network. Inspect each loss term and evaluate on points not used during training. Only then move to sparse observations, unknown parameters, or more complex physics.

The central idea is simple: represent a physical quantity with a trainable function, and constrain that function with what you know about the system. The scientific work lies in choosing valid constraints and showing, with independent evidence, that the learned solution is useful.
