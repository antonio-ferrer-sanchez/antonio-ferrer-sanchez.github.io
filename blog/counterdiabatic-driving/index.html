From solving equations to controlling quantum systems

Counterdiabatic driving in a two-level system: fidelity, control costs and how physics-informed neural networks can learn control protocols.

By Antonio Ferrer-Sánchez

From prediction to control

In the first post, a neural network represented the temperature along a rod. The equation and the initial and boundary conditions specified the problem. Training tried to recover its solution.

Quantum control adds a choice. We can vary an external field, a coupling or another parameter of the Hamiltonian, the operator that governs the system’s evolution. The task is to choose that variation so that the system reaches a desired state. A physics-informed neural network can represent the controls as well as the quantities they affect.

Counterdiabatic driving gives this problem a useful structure. It adds a term to the Hamiltonian that cancels transitions between the instantaneous eigenstates of a reference Hamiltonian. The idea is often described as a shortcut to adiabaticity: preserve the state-following behaviour associated with slow evolution while completing the protocol in a finite, shorter time. Berry’s transitionless-driving construction makes that statement precise.

We will first examine a case that can be solved exactly. Its figures are analytic calculations, checked against direct numerical evolution. They contain no trained PINN results and no quantum-hardware measurements.

A spin in a rotating field

Consider an isolated two-level system, such as an ideal spin one-half. The Pauli operators σx, σy and σz describe its three control directions. Take a field of fixed magnitude whose direction rotates in the x–z plane. Use units with ℏ=1 and gap frequency Ω=1: time is measured in Ω−1 and energy in ℏΩ. The reference Hamiltonian is

H0(t)=12[cosθ(t)σz+sinθ(t)σx],θ(t)=πtT.

Here T is the total duration, and t runs from zero to T. The angle θ increases from zero to π. The two energy levels remain −1/2 and +1/2, so the gap never closes. Their eigenvectors rotate with the field.

Prepare the ground state of the initial Hamiltonian. If the field turns sufficiently slowly compared with the inverse gap, the state stays close to the moving ground state. A faster turn can produce a superposition of the instantaneous ground and excited states. The problem is the changing eigenvectors, even though the energy gap is constant.

The target at the end is the ground state of the final reference Hamiltonian. We also care about the route: a protocol that returns to the target only at the end can have substantial transitions along the way.

Adding the counterdiabatic term

For this rotating field, the exact additional Hamiltonian is particularly simple:

HCD=θ˙2σy=π2Tσy.

The dot denotes a time derivative. We evolve with the sum of the reference and counterdiabatic Hamiltonians. The additional y-directed control compensates for the rotation of the reference eigenvectors. Starting in one of those eigenstates, ideal evolution follows that eigenstate up to a phase.

One way to check the sign and the factor of one half is to move into a frame that rotates with the field. In that frame, the bare dynamics acquires a y term with coefficient −θ˙/2. The counterdiabatic term cancels it, leaving the fixed z Hamiltonian. The state then cannot leave its initial z eigenstate.

The state follows an eigenstate of the reference Hamiltonian. It need not be an instantaneous eigenstate of the total Hamiltonian after the extra control has been added. Keeping these two operators separate avoids a common ambiguity in the phrase “staying in the ground state.”

Compare the same physical task

Let g(t) be the instantaneous reference ground state and ψ(t) the actual evolving state. Their fidelity is

F(t)=|⟨g(t)|ψ(t)⟩|2.

A fidelity of one means that the states agree up to a global phase. Compare three protocols with the same reference path, initial state and duration: no added control, half the exact control, and the full exact control. Write η for the fraction of the counterdiabatic term that is applied, between zero and one, and s=t/T for normalized time. The rotating-frame solution gives

Q=T2+(1−η)2π2,Fη(s)=1−(1−η)2π2Qsin2(sQ2).

At T=2, the final fidelities are approximately 0.347 without the added term, 0.652 at half strength, and 1 with exact counterdiabatic control. The full curves matter: coherent evolution can bring an imperfect protocol back towards the target. A high final fidelity alone does not establish transitionless evolution, and the uncontrolled final fidelity need not increase monotonically with duration.

An independent check integrated the time-dependent Schrödinger equation directly in the fixed Pauli basis with SciPy’s DOP853 solver, using relative tolerance 10−11 and absolute tolerance 10−13. At durations 0.5, 2 and 10 and control fractions 0, 0.5 and 1, the fidelities agreed with the formula to within 2×10−10 on 201 equally spaced times; the state norm passed the same tolerance. These checks validate this example, not the performance of a learned controller.

Faster evolution has a control cost

Exact cancellation does not make a short protocol free. In this example, the operator norm of the extra Hamiltonian equals the magnitude of its Pauli coefficient:

‖ηHCD‖=cη=ηπ2T.

Halving the duration doubles the required strength at a fixed control fraction. The plot shows this dependence directly. It measures a Hamiltonian amplitude, not electrical power or the full experimental cost.

The linear angle sweep also switches a nonzero counterdiabatic control on at the beginning and off at the end. Ideal switching is part of the model. A laboratory pulse must respect finite amplitude and bandwidth, and may require smooth endpoints. Changing the schedule changes the required control waveform and the fidelity curves.

In a many-body system, an exact counterdiabatic operator may require interactions that the device cannot implement. Approximate controls must be assessed under those restrictions. Neither this example nor a low training loss establishes a quantum computational advantage.

Where a neural network helps

The two-level calculation supplies a reference that is easy to test. Harder systems motivate learning: the required operator may be costly to compute, and only a restricted set of controls may be available.

For a general reference Hamiltonian parameterized by λ, introduce the adiabatic gauge potential Aλ. It generates the change of eigenvectors as λ changes. In the same ℏ=1 convention, the control and a useful trial representation are

HCD(t)=λ˙(t)Aλ,Aλ≈∑jaj(λ)Pj.

The operators Pj can be chosen from the implementable Pauli strings. Real coefficients aj keep the trial operator Hermitian. A neural network can output those coefficients as functions of λ. The choice of operator basis sets what the network can represent; a larger network cannot supply an interaction excluded from that basis.

Sels and Polkovnikov developed a variational route to approximate gauge potentials. Define

Gλ=∂λH0+i[Aλ,H0],S(Aλ)=Tr(Gλ2).

The brackets denote a commutator, and the trace is over the system’s Hilbert space. Minimizing this action within the chosen operator family reduces the uncancelled off-diagonal part of Gλ in the energy basis. For an exact gauge potential, Gλ commutes with the reference Hamiltonian; it generally does not vanish, because energy levels can change with λ. A nonzero minimum of the action therefore need not indicate failed training. Components of Aλ that commute with the reference Hamiltonian also require a gauge convention if a unique representation is wanted.

A physics-informed training objective can sample that action at fixed values of λ and optimize the network coefficients without target labels for the exact gauge potential. Trace evaluation itself can become expensive as the Hilbert space grows. The computational cost still needs to be measured.

The schedule λ(t) is another design variable. For instance, a network with parameters w can satisfy the two endpoint values by construction:

λw(s)=s+s(1−s)Nw(s).

For finite network output, this gives λ(0)=0 and λ(1)=1. It does not enforce monotonicity, keep λ between those endpoints, or make the endpoint derivatives vanish. Such requirements need additional structure or explicit constraints. Automatic differentiation supplies the schedule derivative that multiplies the gauge potential.

Learning a gauge potential and choosing a schedule are related but distinct optimization tasks. A schedule objective can include independently evaluated state-transfer error and control costs. The action alone does not determine the best time schedule or certify a final fidelity. If a network also represents the quantum state, its Schrödinger residual, initial condition and normalization must be checked separately.

The connection to our research

Our 2024 paper on PINNs for counterdiabatic quantum computation studies neural representations of the schedule and counterdiabatic operator, using physical constraints that include Hermiticity and a variational action. The journal article demonstrates the method on a two-qubit representation of molecular hydrogen and examines a Pauli-operator decomposition. The accompanying public repository provides code, data, trained models and Jupyter tutorials.

The rotating-field example here is a separate teaching problem. It explains what counterdiabatic control is intended to achieve and provides a reference for checking an implementation. Its exact cancellation should not be read as a result for a trained network, a larger molecular system or a noisy device.

Checks before trusting a learned control

Start with a model whose dynamics you can verify. Fix the initial state, target, duration and allowed control operators before comparing methods. Then inspect both the fidelity over the full trajectory and the final state, together with the amplitude and smoothness of the controls.

Evaluate the learned controls with an independent dynamical solver at times absent from training. Tighten its numerical tolerances, check normalization, and repeat training with different initializations. For a hardware claim, include the device’s actual control restrictions and noise model. For an efficiency claim, count training and validation costs alongside the baseline calculation.

The useful extension of the first post is the role of the unknown function. A network can represent a solution, a control protocol, or an operator that guides the dynamics. Physical constraints make each representation testable; independent evolution establishes what the resulting control actually does.
