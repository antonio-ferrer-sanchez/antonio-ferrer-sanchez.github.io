When smooth neural networks meet shock waves

Why shocks challenge PINNs, how gradient weighting changes the loss, and how conservation checks expose an incorrect wave speed.

By Antonio Ferrer-Sánchez

A sharp front changes the problem

A shock wave concentrates a finite change in a fluid’s state into a very thin region. In an inviscid model, that region becomes a jump. Pressure, density or velocity can have different values on its two sides, and the jump moves as the fluid evolves.

In the introductory post, a physics-informed neural network represented a smoothly diffusing temperature. Automatic differentiation supplied the derivatives in the heat equation. With a shock, the exact field has no ordinary derivative at the jump. The physical law still applies, but we need a formulation that makes sense across the discontinuity.

A network may draw a sharp front in the wrong place. It may also receive a larger pointwise residual while approximating the correct jump more closely. The Burgers equation shows why.

Conservation sets the shock speed

Consider the one-dimensional inviscid Burgers equation. Let u be a dimensionless scalar field, with dimensionless position x and time t. Its flux is quadratic:

∂u∂t+∂f(u)∂x=0,f(u)=u22.

Choose initial data equal to one to the left of the origin and zero to the right. This is a Riemann problem: two constant states separated by a single jump. In smooth regions, the characteristic speed equals the field value. The left state therefore catches the right state, producing a compressive shock.

Integrating the conservation law across a moving jump gives the Rankine–Hugoniot relation. It fixes the speed s from the flux and state on each side:

s=f(uL)−f(uR)uL−uR=uL+uR2=12.

At time 0.6, the shock is at position 0.3, with constant states on either side. The Burgers chapter of the Clawpack Riemann book develops this construction and its characteristic picture.

Conservation also needs an admissibility condition. Reversing the initial states produces an expanding rarefaction, not a compressive shock. A jump can satisfy the Rankine–Hugoniot relation and still select the wrong physical solution. For this convex scalar flux, the entropy condition distinguishes the two cases.

A smooth network can make a steep front

A finite network with smooth activations has a continuous output. It can nevertheless represent a narrow transition. To see how, take a single hyperbolic tangent with prescribed speed v and positive width δ:

uδ(x,t)=12[1−tanh(x−vtδ)].

One tanh unit followed by an affine output represents this function. Its midpoint travels at the chosen speed, and reducing the width makes the transition steeper.

The narrower curve approaches the step at every point away from the front and reduces the integrated absolute error. Convergence is not uniform: arbitrarily close to the jump, the smooth transition still contains intermediate values.

Now substitute the smooth profile into the equation. Because the profile is a travelling wave, its time derivative is minus its speed times its spatial derivative. The residual becomes

r=∂uδ∂t+uδ∂uδ∂x=(uδ−v)∂uδ∂x.

The spatial derivative grows as the width decreases. Even at the correct speed, the residual is nonzero inside the smooth layer. Its positive and negative contributions cancel when integrated across the whole front, but their squares do not cancel. A squared pointwise residual can therefore grow as the profile becomes sharper.

What gradient weighting changes

GA-PINNs reduce the influence of steep gradients through a weight λ. For a single dimensionless field, let g=|∂xuδ| and use

λ(g)=11+αgβ,Jλ=∫−∞∞λ(g)r2dx.

The strength α sets how much the gradient affects the weight; the exponent β controls its shape. For positive strength, the weight approaches one in flat regions and zero at large gradients. For dimensional fields, the gradients need corresponding scales. This factor appears in the GA-PINN formulation, which combines density, velocity and pressure gradients.

Adjust the controls to follow the weight from gradient space to the shock front, then compare the weighted residual and its spatial integral. The dotted integral curves use the correct speed, 0.5.

Weighting reduces the penalty on sharp fronts, but a small weighted residual does not guarantee the correct propagation speed. Initial and boundary conditions remain part of the training objective.

For the incorrect speed, the midpoint reaches 0.42 at time 0.6, giving a position error of 0.12 regardless of how thin the layer becomes. Integrating the unweighted residual gives 0.2, the same defect obtained from the jump’s flux balance.

The connection to GA-PINNs

Our 2024 GA-PINNs paper uses gradient-dependent weighting and adaptive activation slopes for relativistic Riemann problems. The network predicts density, velocity and pressure. The study compares its solutions with exact references, a baseline PINN and a shock-capturing scheme. The public repository provides the research implementation.

Checks that a loss curve cannot replace

  1. Locate the waves. Compare shock positions at several times and estimate their propagation speeds. For this monotone scalar example, use the half-height crossing to locate the front.
  2. Check the jump balance. Evaluate the flux and state on both sides. The Rankine–Hugoniot defect should decrease with a better solution. For a system, check every conserved component.
  3. Measure the conservation budget. On a fixed interval, the change in the integrated field must equal the net flux through the boundaries.
  4. Resolve the transition in the evaluation grid. Report a field error on points separate from training, together with front position and overshoot. Refine that grid until the diagnostics are stable.
  5. Check physical admissibility. Distinguish shocks from rarefactions. In hydrodynamics, also check density, pressure and any applicable velocity bounds.

For the Burgers example, the conservation budget on a fixed interval is

ddt∫abu(x,t)dx=f(u(a,t))−f(u(b,t)).

While the endpoints remain in the constant left and right states, the net incoming flux is 0.5. The amount of field inside the interval should increase at that rate. A sharp jump moving at speed 0.7 instead increases it at rate 0.7. This gives the same 0.2 imbalance without differentiating through the front.

Comparisons with a classical solver should use the same initial and boundary conditions and a reference checked under mesh refinement. Compare errors at matched times and include the cost of training. For neural results, report variation across initializations as well as the best run.

A useful first experiment

Begin with a Riemann problem whose solution is known. Keep the network, initial data and computational budget fixed while changing the residual weighting. Compare the predicted profiles, wave trajectories and conservation budgets.
