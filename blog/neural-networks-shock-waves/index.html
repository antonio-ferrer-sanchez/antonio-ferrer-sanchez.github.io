Relativistic fluids: teaching neural networks to follow shocks

Density, pressure and velocity in a relativistic shock tube: explore the Euler equations in Minkowski space and how PINNs learn fluid evolution.

By Antonio Ferrer-Sánchez

When the fluid is relativistic

A pressure imbalance sets a fluid in motion. At relativistic speeds, its evolution couples rest mass, momentum and energy through the Lorentz factor. Pressure and internal energy also contribute to the fluid’s inertia. The familiar Euler equations must account for these effects.

Here we consider a perfect fluid in Minkowski spacetime: flat spacetime, with no gravitational source terms in Cartesian coordinates. A one-dimensional shock tube lets us see the resulting waves in density, pressure and velocity, then ask how a physics-informed neural network can represent their evolution.

The equations in flat spacetime

Let ρ be the rest-mass density measured with the fluid, p its pressure and uμ its four-velocity. We set c=1 and use metric signature (−,+,+,+). Rest-mass conservation and energy–momentum conservation are

∂μ(ρuμ)=0,∂μTμν=0Tμν=ρhuμuν+pημν

Here ημν is the Minkowski metric. The perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor contains the specific enthalpy h. For an ideal gas with adiabatic index Γ, the closure is

uμ=W(1,vx,vy,vz)W=11−v2h=1+ε+pρ,p=(Γ−1)ρε

Here ε is specific internal energy, and v2 is the squared three-speed. The density ρ is a rest-frame quantity; the velocity and the conserved densities below refer to the chosen laboratory frame.

For motion along one Cartesian direction, with no transverse velocity, the system becomes

∂U∂t+∂F∂x=0
U=(DSτ),F=(DvSv+pS−Dv)D=ρW,S=ρhW2vτ=ρhW2−p−D

The three components are laboratory-frame rest-mass density D, momentum density S and energy density excluding rest-mass energy τ. Their fluxes describe what crosses a fixed surface. The Lorentz factor couples the primitive variables to all three conserved quantities. Martí and Müller’s review develops this formulation and its numerical solution.

Open a relativistic shock tube

Initially, two uniform fluids are at rest, separated at x=0. The left side is denser and has higher pressure. Choose Γ=53 and the following states:

Initial state Rest-mass density Pressure Velocity
Left 1 1 0
Right 0.125 0.1 0

Density is measured in units of the initial left density, pressure in that density times c2, and velocity in units of c. Position uses an arbitrary length scale; the time unit is the time light takes to cross that length.

Remove the separation and the pressure imbalance drives the flow. The surfaces sample the exact Riemann solution over position and time. Select a fluid variable, rotate the view, and move the time slider to inspect its profile.

Read the three waves

The initial jump separates into three features:

  • Rarefaction. An expanding fan travels into the high-pressure left state. Density and pressure decrease smoothly through it while the fluid accelerates to the right.
  • Contact discontinuity. Two fluids meet at the same pressure and velocity but with different densities. The contact moves with the fluid.
  • Shock. A sharp compression propagates into the right state. Density, pressure and velocity change across it.

The contact moves at approximately 0.437 times the speed of light; the shock moves at 0.819. The wave pattern can therefore travel faster than the material behind it. This distinction is clear in the velocity panel: the fluid speed between the rarefaction and shock is about 0.437, not the shock speed.

Across a shock, ordinary derivatives are insufficient. Integrating the conservation laws through the moving front gives

[F]=s[U]

Brackets denote the difference between right and left states, and s is the front speed. All three balances must hold. The physical shock also satisfies the entropy condition. The relativistic Riemann construction combines these jump conditions with the smooth rarefaction solution.

What the neural network learns

A PINN takes position and time as inputs and predicts the primitive variables:

Nθ(t,x)=(ρθ,pθ,vθ)Rθ=∂Uθ∂t+∂Fθ∂x

These outputs determine the Lorentz factor, enthalpy, conserved state and flux. Automatic differentiation then evaluates the time and space derivatives in the Euler residuals. Training combines those residuals with the known initial states and the boundary conditions, and updates the network parameters through backpropagation.

For this time interval, the waves stay inside the domain, so its ends remain in their initial states. The output parameterization can enforce positive density and pressure and a speed below light speed. Such bounds help keep the prediction physically admissible; the conservation equations still have to be learned.

Predicting primitive variables directly is convenient: a conventional conservative solver must recover them from the evolved conserved state, which requires a nonlinear inversion in relativistic hydrodynamics. A neural representation avoids that inversion at evaluation, but introduces a training problem of its own.

Learning across a shock

A smooth network can approximate a steep front. Near a discontinuity, however, a pointwise squared residual can penalize the large derivatives even when the front approaches the correct solution. A small training loss alone also does not establish the correct wave speed.

Our GA-PINNs paper studies gradient-dependent residual weighting and adaptive activation slopes for relativistic Riemann problems. It gives the algorithm, comparisons and numerical results; the public repository contains the implementation.

After training, check the predicted wave positions and all three conservation budgets on points separate from training. Check density and pressure positivity, the velocity bound, and the jump balance across the shock. The exact shock-tube solution provides a reference for each of these tests, including the contact that appears in density but not in pressure or velocity.
